Most practices build their first campaign by guessing. They pick a treatment they want more of, write
an ad they think sounds good, and run it. Then they find out what the market already knew.

There is a free tool that skips most of that. The Meta Ad Library shows you every ad currently running
on Facebook and Instagram, by any advertiser, in any country — including every practice within driving
distance of yours. You can see their offer, their hook, their creative, and the page they send patients
to, before you spend anything.

This is a research guide, not a swipe file. The point is understanding what your market is testing and
how practices near you position treatment.

## The tool

Go to **[facebook.com/ads/library](https://www.facebook.com/ads/library)**. There is no account
required and nothing to install.

Search by advertiser to see everything one practice is running, or by keyword to see who is bidding on
a treatment across a whole country. Set the country filter first — the library is region-scoped, and
the default will not be your market.

> **A limit worth knowing up front.** For ordinary commercial ads, Meta shows you the creative and the
> run dates, but not spend, impressions or results. Political and social-issue ads carry that data;
> dental ads do not. So you can see *what* is running and *how long* it has been running — never how
> well it worked.

## How to read what you find

The run date is the closest thing to a performance signal you get. Every ad in the library shows when
it started running.

An ad that has been live for four months is a much more interesting object than one that launched last
week. Nobody keeps paying for something that loses money, so longevity is weak evidence that a campaign
is at least breaking even. Weak, not proof — some practices genuinely do leave bad ads running.

Treat it as a ranking signal for your attention. Read the six-month-old ad closely. Skim the new one.

The other thing to notice is **how many versions of the same ad an advertiser is running.** A practice
running one static image is dabbling. A practice running eleven variations of the same offer with
different opening lines is testing systematically, and is worth far more of your time.

## What to audit

Do not just look at whether the ad is pretty. Break down the business behind it. There are five things
on the ad itself.

### 1. The treatment

What are they actually trying to sell? Implants, clear aligners, veneers, whitening, new-patient exams,
emergency dentistry.

This is worth writing down because it is rarely what a practice says it does. A practice whose website
leads with "comprehensive family dentistry" and whose ads all sell implants has told you where the
margin is.

### 2. The hook

The first line, the first three seconds, the thing on the thumbnail. Write it down exactly — not your
summary of it, the actual words.

Then classify it. Is it leading with pain, desire, price, speed, fear, convenience, proof, or
curiosity? Once you have twenty hooks written out, the classification is where the pattern shows up.
Most dental markets are saturated on price and starved on proof.

### 3. The offer

What makes the patient act now rather than later? Free exam, complimentary scan, financing, a limited
event, a bundled service, a same-day option — or simply a strong educational offer.

Note whether the offer is a discount or a reduction in risk. "£99 whitening" and "free 15-minute
consult, no obligation" both lower the barrier, but they attract different patients and they set very
different expectations about what your practice is.

### 4. The creative

Doctor talking. Before and after. Patient testimonial. Animation. Procedure footage. Stock creative.
Text graphic. Office footage.

Note who appears on camera. A market where every ad is stock footage is a market where one practice
putting an actual clinician on screen will stand out for very little money.

### 5. The CTA

What exact action are they asking for? Book. Call. Learn more. Send a message. Claim offer. Schedule
exam.

The gap between "learn more" and "book now" is a whole strategy. One collects people who are curious;
the other collects people who are ready, and far fewer of them.

## The part most dentists miss

Click the ad.

Almost nobody does this, and it is where most of the information is. The ad is the cheap half of a
campaign; the page it points at is where the money is actually won or lost.

### 6. The landing page

Look for the headline, the offer restated, doctor credibility, before-and-after cases, testimonials,
financing, FAQs, and the booking flow.

Then count clicks. How many taps from the ad to being a booked lead? Three is good. Six means they are
losing most of the traffic they paid for, and that is a gap you can beat without outspending anyone.

### 7. The message match

Does the landing page continue the exact promise made in the ad?

If the ad says "implant exam", the page should immediately continue that implant conversation. A
surprising number of practices spend real money sending implant traffic to a generic homepage, where
the patient has to find their own way to the thing they clicked for. Most of them do not.

### 8. Repeated patterns

After five or ten practices, stop looking at individual ads and look across them. Which treatments keep
showing up? Which hooks repeat? Which offers are common? Which creative styles are everywhere?

> **Patterns do not prove something is profitable.** They tell you what the market is testing and where
> to investigate further. A whole market can be wrong at the same time — and when it is, the gap it
> leaves is the most valuable thing on this page.

## Where to start

Five to ten practices is enough for the patterns to appear. Pick:

- Two or three direct competitors — the practices a patient would choose instead of you.
- Two strong practices in another city, far enough away that they are not competing with you.
- One DSO or corporate group, which will be the most systematic advertiser on your list.
- One specialist centre in the treatment you most want to grow.

Work through the eight points above for each. Budget about ten minutes per practice once you have done
two or three and know what you are looking for.

## The rule

Do not copy your competitor's ad. Copy the discipline of researching before guessing.

An ad you copy carries assumptions you cannot see — about their patient list, their capacity, their
pricing, their location. What transfers is the method, not the creative. Your goal is to understand the
market, find the gaps, and build a stronger angle for your own practice.
